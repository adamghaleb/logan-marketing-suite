This is a template, not a finished plan. The point is to walk into the room with a frame for the first three months — three priorities, each with a metric or a visible artifact at the end of it. You'll fill in real specifics after the conversation; that's the whole reason it's a draft.

Lead with **audience**, not channel. That's the Logan tell. If your priorities sound like "learn the channels, build the dashboards, run the campaigns," you're pitching for a different agency.

## How to use this page

- **Before the interview:** read it once. Don't memorize. Pick one priority you genuinely care about and rehearse it as a 90-second answer.
- **In the room:** if they ask "where would you start?", answer with priorities, not a checklist. One of yours, not all three.
- **After the offer:** rewrite the whole thing with real client names, real metrics, and what you actually heard them say in the interview.

## The frame

Three priorities. Each has an outcome (what changes), a method (how you'd get there), and a proof (what someone else can see at the end of the period).

### Days 0–30 — Get into the audience

|             |                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Outcome** | I can describe Logan's three biggest accounts the way an insider would.                                                                                                                              |
| **Method**  | Read every page on each client's site. Read the last 60 days of their socials. Read the brief if there is one. Sit on every internal meeting I'm invited to. Take notes in their language, not mine. |
| **Proof**   | A short audience snapshot for each account — one page, plain English, the kind of thing a strategist could hand to a designer.                                                                       |

The thing you're trying to avoid in month one is generic competence. Anyone can be useful in a meeting. The version of you they'll remember is the one who can describe the audience for one of their clients better than a junior should be able to.

### Days 30–60 — Get into the work

|             |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Outcome** | I've contributed to live work on at least two accounts and I know the shop's standards from the inside.                                                                                                                              |
| **Method**  | Take whatever's handed to me. Ship it carefully. Get feedback. Notice the patterns in the feedback — what gets cut, what gets praised, what gets quietly fixed by someone senior without saying anything. Write those patterns down. |
| **Proof**   | A small body of shipped work, with one piece I'd defend in a portfolio review.                                                                                                                                                       |

The mistake to avoid here is performing — over-iterating on your own pieces, asking for feedback you don't actually need, treating every task like a referendum on your hire. Just do the work. Ship cleanly. Be the easy one to work with.

### Days 60–90 — Get into the rhythm

|             |                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Outcome** | I'm anticipating the next step on the accounts I'm on, not waiting to be told.                                                                                              |
| **Method**  | Start volunteering one thing per week — a content angle, a copy fix, an audience observation, a question I'd want to ask the client. Push them lightly. Be wrong sometimes. |
| **Proof**   | A small idea I floated that ended up in a real piece of work. Even a tiny one.                                                                                              |

By day 90 the goal isn't to be senior. The goal is to be **trusted with the next thing**. People get trusted when they show up with opinions before they're asked.

## What's not on this list

- **No "build my personal brand" goal.** Don't even think about it for a year. The work is the brand for now.
- **No "get promoted" goal.** Promotions follow trust, not plans.
- **No metrics dashboard for yourself.** You're not a campaign. The proof points above are enough.

## Sample one-liner for the interview

If they ask "how would you approach the first 90 days?", here's a single answer that holds the whole frame:

> "Audience first — I'd want the first month to be all reading and listening, getting close enough to your three or four biggest accounts that I could describe their customers the way you would. Month two I'd want to be shipping on real work and learning the shop's standards from inside the feedback. Month three I'd want to start volunteering ideas, even small ones, before they're asked for. The thing I'd be measuring myself on at 90 days isn't output. It's whether I've earned the right to push back on a brief."

You don't have to say it exactly like that. But that's the shape.
