Logan writes warm, observational, lightly editorial. Long-form captions, full sentences, no exclamation-point spam. Earnest, not ironic. They sound like someone who actually knows the brand they're writing about and isn't trying to be the loudest voice in the feed.

The trick of their voice is restraint. Most agency writing performs effort — adjective stacks, hype words, "best-in-class." Logan trims. The good Logan sentence is specific, sensory, and one beat shorter than you'd expect.

If you mirror this in the interview — even in how you describe your own past work — you'll sound like you already work there.

## The shape of a Logan sentence

- **One concrete detail** beats three abstract claims.
- **Sensory before strategic** — the texture of the thing, then the reason it works.
- **Editorial confidence**, not pitch-deck swagger. They state; they don't sell.
- **Long-form is fine.** A 90-word caption is normal if every sentence earns its place.

## Do / don't

| Don't write                                                        | Write instead                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "A best-in-class brand experience that delivers on every channel." | "We rebuilt the homepage around the one thing first-time visitors actually do: read about the founders." |
| "Stunning visuals tell a powerful story."                          | "The shoot is grain, low light, the kind of breakfast you'd actually photograph."                        |
| "Synergy between strategy and creative."                           | "The strategist sat in on the shoot. That's how the line ended up on the label."                         |
| "🚀 Big news! 🎉 We launched!"                                     | "The site is live. The hardest sentence to write was the first one on the About page."                   |
| "Innovative, results-driven solutions."                            | "A brand that already knew what it was, written down so a new hire could find it."                       |
| "Click here to learn more!"                                        | "The full case study is on the work page."                                                               |

## Words they probably use

Pulled from observation of their public writing. Useful for the interview without sounding like you swallowed a thesaurus.

- **Audience** (more than "users" or "consumers")
- **Brief**, **brand**, **voice**, **frame**, **angle**
- **Operator**, **founder**, **team** (people-focused, not org-chart-focused)
- **Sharpen**, **tighten**, **earn**, **trim**
- **Specific** as a compliment

## Words they probably don't use

If one of these slips into the interview, you'll feel the room cool by half a degree.

- _Leverage_ as a verb
- _Ideation_, _ideate_
- _Best-in-class_, _world-class_, _next-gen_
- _Disruptive_, _game-changing_, _innovative_
- _Stack_ used as a strategy noun ("our brand stack")
- _Drive_ as a verb of growth ("drive engagement")
- "Stunning," "breathtaking," "luxurious" — even for hospitality work

## Practice line

Take your favorite project from your portfolio. Describe it in two sentences using:

1. One concrete detail that only someone who worked on it would know.
2. The reason that detail mattered for the audience.

If the second sentence has the word "leverage" in it, rewrite it.

## What to do in the room

You don't need to perform their voice. You need to **not perform anyone's voice.** That alone gets you 80% of the way to sounding like Logan.

Speak in full sentences. Leave a beat after a question instead of rushing into your answer. Use one sensory detail per story you tell. Don't say "amazing" once.
