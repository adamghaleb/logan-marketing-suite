Five questions, prepared, written down, used at the end of the interview when they ask "do you have any questions for us?" Two strategic, two team/culture, one weird-but-good. None of them are answerable from the careers page.

The point isn't to fire all five. It's to have them ready, pick the two or three that fit the conversation that actually happened, and ask them in a way that sounds like real curiosity — because they are.

Read the question, then the **why I'm asking** note underneath it. The note is for you, not for the room.

## Strategic — 1

> **"What kind of work has Logan turned down in the last year that you wish you could have said yes to?"**

**Why I'm asking:** this surfaces their real positioning. Every shop has a list of accounts they passed on for size, fit, or principle reasons. The answer tells you where their line is — and which side of it they wish they were on. It's a question a peer would ask, not a candidate.

**What you're listening for:** whether they sound principled, ambitious, conflicted, or rationalizing. All four are useful information.

## Strategic — 2

> **"When you think about a Logan project that worked exactly the way it was supposed to, what's the part that almost no one outside the shop would notice?"**

**Why I'm asking:** this is a way to ask "what makes you good?" without flattery. It pulls out the small craft details — a strategist who sat in on the shoot, a copywriter who pushed back on a brief, a research call that changed the brand voice halfway through. Those details are the actual answer to "should I work here?"

**What you're listening for:** specificity. If they answer in generic agency platitudes ("our team's collaboration"), the shop might be a brand on top of a less interesting middle. If they answer with a specific moment, you've found the real thing.

## Team / culture — 1

> **"How does feedback flow between strategy, creative, and growth here? Like — when those three teams disagree, who's usually right, and how do you find out?"**

**Why I'm asking:** in a brand-led shop the strategy/creative/growth dynamic is the whole show. Asking _who's usually right_ is half-joke, half-serious — it surfaces who has the cultural high ground inside the shop. That's load-bearing information for a junior trying to figure out which lane to learn in first.

**What you're listening for:** whether they describe a healthy creative tension or a dominant function that runs the building. Either is fine. You just want to know.

## Team / culture — 2

> **"What's a habit a junior here picks up in the first six months that they don't even notice they've picked up?"**

**Why I'm asking:** this is the real culture question, dressed up. It pulls out the unwritten norms — how they write, how they argue, what they share, what they don't — without asking "tell me about your culture," which gets you a poster.

**What you're listening for:** anything specific. A writing convention, a meeting ritual, a Slack reaction, a time-of-day everyone goes quiet. Specifics tell you what the place actually feels like.

## Weird-but-good — 1

> **"What's a brand you don't work with that you read every piece of work from anyway?"**

**Why I'm asking:** asking what someone reads is the fastest way to find out who they want to be. It also signals that you're the kind of person who reads, which is the cheapest, most accurate proxy for "will be good at this job."

**What you're listening for:** a real answer with a real name. If they have to think about it, you've learned something. If they fire off three names without pausing, you've found people you might genuinely enjoy working with.

## How to actually use these in the room

- **Don't read them.** Glance at the page beforehand, then close it. The questions need to come out in your voice.
- **Pick two or three**, not five. Five questions in the closing minutes is a quiz, not a conversation.
- **Ask the strategic ones earlier**, in any natural opening — they double as "I'm thinking about this seriously" signals when they come up mid-conversation. Save the weird-but-good one for last; it leaves the room warm.
- **Listen to the answer for an extra beat** before asking the next thing. The follow-up question is usually the real one. Most candidates don't ask follow-ups, and it's a noticeable difference.

## A note on what not to ask

Skip these even if you're tempted:

- Anything answerable from the careers page (benefits, vacation, hybrid policy — save for the offer stage).
- "What's the company culture like?" — generic; you'll get a generic answer.
- "What are the biggest challenges the team is facing?" — feels like a consulting question, not a peer question.
- "When will I hear back?" — fine to ask the recruiter, not fine to ask the founder.
